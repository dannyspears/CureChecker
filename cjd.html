Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no cure or approved treatment for Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease anywhere in the world, and every past drug trial has failed. CJD moves very fast, often in months. Our view: the most realistic path is lowering the prion protein the body makes, the "fuel" the disease needs. That's likely to work best in people with genetic prion disease who are treated before symptoms start.

  1. Prion-protein lowering: the leading strategy.ION717, an antisense drug, is in a Phase 1/2 trial in the U.S., Germany and France. The protein drop so far was smaller than hoped, so a higher dose was added in 2026.
  2. A second approach has entered the clinic.The PRiSM trial of an siRNA drug started recruiting in April 2026. Its lead investigator is Eric Minikel, who turned to prion research with his wife Sonia Vallabh after she learned she carries a prion-disease gene.
  3. Prevention for gene carriers: the real cure target.About 10–15% of prion disease is inherited, and a blood test can find carriers years before symptoms. Treating them early could prevent the disease entirely.
  4. Faster diagnosis: buying time.The RT-QuIC test can confirm CJD from spinal fluid within days. Earlier diagnosis is essential if any future drug is going to help.

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. Care decisions belong with a neurologist. The CJD Foundation HelpLine and national prion centers can connect families with specialists.

Treatments being tested

Nothing has been shown to slow CJD yet. For the first time, though, drugs designed specifically against prion disease are being tested in people, alongside better care to keep patients comfortable.

ION717

Phase 1/2a
Ionis · antisense drug · PrProfile trial · U.S., Germany, France

Injected into spinal fluid to cut production of the prion protein. It looked safe and acted on its target, but lowered the protein less than expected, so a higher dose was added in 2026. Main results are expected in 2027.

PRiSM (siRNA)

Phase 1 recruiting
Broad Institute & UMass Chan · first-in-human · Apr 2026

A different gene-silencing approach that also lowers prion protein. This first trial checks safety and whether protein levels drop.

PRN100 antibody

Compassionate use
UCL / UCLH (UK) · 6 patients · Lancet Neurology 2022

A world-first antibody against prion protein. It was safe and reached the brain, and disease may have stabilized in some patients while levels were on target. It hasn't been tested in a full trial.

RT-QuIC testing

In use
Diagnosis · spinal fluid, skin and nasal samples

Finds misfolded prions with about 96% sensitivity and 100% specificity in spinal fluid. Faster, surer diagnosis matters for families and for any future treatment.

Quinacrine

Did not work
Antimalarial · tested in the 2000s

Seemed promising in the lab, but compassionate use and the UK PRION-1 trial showed no meaningful effect on survival.

Doxycycline, pentosan

Did not work
Repurposed drugs

Neither showed a clear benefit in controlled studies of CJD. They're not recommended outside research.

Curcumin, green tea, other antioxidants

Some lab interest only. There's no human evidence in prion disease.

"Prion cures" sold online

There are none. Be wary of anyone selling a treatment for CJD outside a registered trial.

How it changed over time
  1. 1920sGermany: Hans Creutzfeldt and Alfons Jakob first describe the disease.
  2. 1996UK: variant CJD is linked to "mad cow" disease (BSE), and national surveillance is expanded.
  3. 2009UK: the PRION-1 trial finds quinacrine doesn't work.
  4. 2015RT-QuIC enters clinical use and transforms diagnosis.
  5. 2022UK: first-in-human PRN100 antibody results are published.
  6. 2023U.S. and Europe: the ION717 trial begins, the first prion-lowering drug tested in people.

What causes CJD

CJD is a prion disease. A normal brain protein called the prion protein (PrP) folds into the wrong shape. The misfolded protein then forces nearby normal copies to misfold too, in a chain reaction that rapidly destroys brain tissue.

Step 1 · How it starts

Three ways in

~85% sporadic~10–15% genetic · <1% acquired
  • Sporadic: appears by chance, with no known cause. Typical onset is around 60–70.
  • Genetic: inherited PRNP gene changes (including fatal familial insomnia and GSS)
  • Acquired: very rare. Contaminated medical procedures, or variant CJD from BSE ("mad cow") beef.
Step 2 · The tipping point

A misfolding chain reaction

  • Each misfolded prion acts as a template that converts more normal PrP
  • That's why less normal PrP could slow or prevent the disease
Step 3 · The damage

Brain tissue turns spongy

  • Cell death: neurons die quickly across the brain
  • Holes: the tissue looks sponge-like under the microscope ("spongiform")
  • Hard to destroy: prions resist normal sterilization, which is why hospitals take special precautions
Step 4 · The result

Rapid decline

  • Fast-moving dementia, unsteadiness, muscle jerks and vision problems
  • Most people with sporadic CJD die within a year of symptoms
  • It affects about 1–2 people per million each year
Diagnosis got faster: the RT-QuIC test detects misfolded prions in spinal fluid with very high accuracy. It's now part of the U.S. CDC's criteria for probable CJD.
Why genetics matters: people who carry a PRNP mutation can be identified years in advance. That makes them the first realistic group for prevention trials.
Not contagious in daily life: CJD doesn't spread by touch, air or caring for someone. Families can hug, feed and care for their loved one.
