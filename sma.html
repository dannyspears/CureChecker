Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no cure for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). It happens when both copies of the SMN1 gene are faulty, so the nerve cells that control muscles slowly die. Since 2016 the FDA has approved medicines that raise the missing SMN protein: Spinraza, Zolgensma, Evrysdi and, in November 2025, Itvisma. They can slow or stop the disease, and results are best when treatment starts before symptoms, which is why every U.S. state now screens newborns. On September 11, 2026 the FDA added Isembyld, which works on muscle instead. Our view: these treatments change the outlook, but they don't bring back nerve cells already lost, so a cure would need to repair that damage too.

  1. A new kind of medicine.Isembyld (apitegromab) was approved on September 11, 2026 for people 2 and older who already take nusinersen or risdiplam. It blocks myostatin, a protein that limits muscle growth.
  2. Gene therapy for older children and adults.Itvisma, a one-time gene therapy given into the spinal fluid, was approved in the U.S. on November 24, 2025 and in the EU on June 30, 2026, for ages 2 and older.
  3. A stronger Spinraza dose.A high-dose Spinraza schedule was approved in the EU on January 12, 2026 and by the FDA on March 27, 2026.
  4. Earlier is better.In the SPR1NT trial, all 14 babies treated before symptoms survived without a permanent breathing machine. All 50 states have screened newborns for SMA since January 2024.

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. Care decisions belong with an SMA-experienced medical team.

A possible missing link

Please read first. This is an AI-generated hypothesis, written by Cure Checker's AI from published research. It is speculation, not an established finding. It is not a recommendation, not a prediction that a cure exists, and not medical advice. No doctors or scientists have reviewed it, and it may be wrong. Never start, stop, change or delay any treatment because of it. Talk with your SMA care team.

What looks like it is working

  • Treating before symptoms. In the SPR1NT trial, all 14 babies with two SMN2 copies given gene therapy in their first six weeks learned to sit, 11 at the usual age (Nat Med, 2022).
  • Newborn screening at scale. In Turkey, among babies with two SMN2 copies treated with nusinersen, 6.3% of those found by screening died, against 30.9% diagnosed before screening began (Eur J Paediatr Neurol, 2026). It was a records study, not randomized.
  • One child treated before birth. A mother took risdiplam for the last six weeks of pregnancy, and the child took it after birth. At 30 months the child had no signs of SMA, but had a developmental delay and other differences doctors think began before the drug (NEJM, 2025). This is one child, not proof.

Where the links are missing

  • Damage may start before birth. Even treated before symptoms, some babies with two SMN2 copies had delays or needed breathing or feeding support (Int J Neonatal Screen, 2024). In infants who died of severe SMA, and in mice, motor nerve fibers failed to mature properly; in mice only treatment in the womb fixed this (Sci Transl Med, 2021). Our inference: in severe cases, treatment at birth may already be late.
  • No tested way to treat before birth. Apart from that one child, it has been tried only in animals: a drug injected into the fluid around mouse and sheep fetuses (Sci Transl Med, 2025), and a risdiplam-like drug given to pregnant mice, which helped only partly when stopped at birth (Ann Neurol, 2026). A review says these methods need more safety work first (Clin Ther, 2026). Our inference: we found no registered trial of treatment before birth, though we may have missed one.

Our hypothesis

For babies with only two SMN2 copies, could part of the damage that newborn treatment cannot undo happen in the last weeks before birth? If so, a carefully studied treatment that starts before birth and continues after it might help more of these children develop like their peers. Treatment during pregnancy is not an approved use and should only be explored in carefully run research.

What would prove it wrong: if babies with two SMN2 copies treated before birth do no better in movement than similar babies treated in their first days of life.

Hypothesis written October 6, 2026. Cure Checker's AI will revisit it when major new evidence appears.

Treatments that are working

Four FDA-approved medicines raise SMN protein, and one newer medicine builds on them by working on muscle. None is a cure. Supportive care for breathing, feeding and movement is still essential.

Spinraza (nusinersen)

FDA approved
Approved Dec 23, 2016 · children and adults · spinal injection

Helps the SMN2 backup gene make more working SMN protein. It is given into the spinal fluid every 4 months after loading doses. A high-dose schedule (two 50 mg loading doses, then 28 mg every 4 months) was approved by the FDA on March 27, 2026.

Zolgensma (onasemnogene abeparvovec)

FDA approved
Approved May 24, 2019 · under age 2 · one-time IV infusion

A gene therapy that delivers a working copy of the SMN gene. It carries a boxed warning for serious liver injury, so liver tests are needed for at least three months after treatment.

Evrysdi (risdiplam)

FDA approved
Approved Aug 7, 2020 · children and adults · taken by mouth daily

A daily medicine that boosts SMN protein from SMN2. A 5 mg tablet was approved on February 11, 2025, in addition to the liquid. The most common side effects in later-onset SMA were fever, diarrhea and rash.

Itvisma (onasemnogene abeparvovec-brve)

FDA approved
Approved Nov 24, 2025 · ages 2 and older · one-time spinal injection

The same gene therapy as Zolgensma at one fixed dose, given into the spinal fluid. In the Phase 3 STEER trial (126 people aged 2–17 who could sit but had never walked), motor scores improved by 1.88 points more than with a sham procedure.

Isembyld (apitegromab)

FDA approved
Approved Sep 11, 2026 · ages 2+ already on an SMN2 treatment · IV every 4 weeks

The first SMA medicine aimed at muscle. In the SAPPHIRE trial, 34.2% of children aged 2–12 on the approved dose improved by 3 or more points on a motor scale, compared with 13.5% on placebo. The label warns of serious bone fractures.

Natural remedies & daily care

No supplement treats SMA. The daily care that helps most is physical therapy, good nutrition and support for breathing and the spine. We rate each by the strength of evidence.

Strong expert consensus Limited / mixed evidence No evidence, or possible harm

Helpful daily care

Physical therapy, stretching & positioning

International SMA care guidelines recommend regular therapy, stretching, bracing and positioning to protect joints and function.

Dietitian-guided nutrition

The same guidelines call for checks on growth, swallowing and feeding, since both under- and overweight are common.

Exercise & breathing-muscle training

A Cochrane review found the evidence on exercise in SMA type 3 too weak to judge. A small 2025 trial of breathing-muscle training found it safe, with only a small gain in strength.

Promoted without evidence, or risky

Creatine

A Cochrane review found creatine probably has no meaningful effect on motor function in SMA types 2 and 3.

Valproic acid with L-carnitine

Tested in trials and found to probably have no meaningful benefit. Valproic acid is a prescription drug with its own risks.

Other repurposed compounds

Gabapentin, hydroxyurea and phenylbutyrate also showed no meaningful benefit in trials reviewed by Cochrane.

Be careful: don't delay or stop an approved SMA treatment in favor of a supplement. Tell your SMA team about every supplement you take, because swallowing problems and other medicines can change what's safe.

What causes spinal muscular atrophy

SMA is inherited. A child gets it when both copies of the SMN1 gene don't work. Without enough SMN protein, motor neurons in the spinal cord die, and the muscles they control weaken.

Step 1 · The gene

Both SMN1 copies fail

~1 in 8,000–10,000people
  • Inherited: autosomal recessive, so each parent usually carries one faulty copy
  • The gene: SMN1 on chromosome 5 makes survival motor neuron (SMN) protein
  • Types 0 to 4: type 1, the severe infant form, is about half of cases
Step 2 · The tipping point

Too little SMN protein

  • A backup gene, SMN2, makes some SMN protein, but most of it doesn't work
  • More SMN2 copies usually means milder disease. Most current medicines target SMN2
Step 3 · The damage

Motor neurons die

  • Where: nerve cells in the spinal cord that signal muscles
  • Type 1: starts in early infancy; babies may never sit without help
  • Types 2 and 3: start later in childhood with milder weakness
  • Type 4: starts in adulthood
Step 4 · The result

Muscles weaken

  • Weakness can affect moving, swallowing and breathing
  • The FDA has called SMA a leading genetic cause of infant death
The gene is found (January 1995): researchers reported in the journal Cell that SMN1 is the gene that causes SMA, which later made gene-targeted medicines possible.
Newborn screening added (July 2018): the U.S. federal government added SMA to its Recommended Uniform Screening Panel, the list of conditions states should test newborns for.
Every state screening (January 2024): Hawaii and Nevada became the last two states to start testing newborns for SMA, so babies born anywhere in the U.S. are now checked at birth.
