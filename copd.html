Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no cure for COPD, and lung damage can't yet be reversed. But COPD is highly preventable and treatable. Quitting smoking, inhalers, pulmonary rehab and vaccines all change how people do, and since 2024 biologic injections have cut flare-ups in specific groups. Our view: progress will come from matching treatment to each person's type of inflammation, from preventing COPD in the first place, and one day from repairing lung tissue.

  1. Biologics for "type 2" COPD: here now.Dupilumab (2024) and mepolizumab (2025) reduce flare-ups in people with high blood eosinophils, a type of white blood cell.
  2. Reaching everyone else: the next wave.Tozorakimab, which blocks the IL-33 signal, cut flare-ups by 29–34% in two Phase 3 trials, including in people with low eosinophils. The FDA's decision is due in early 2027. A rival IL-33 drug, itepekimab, failed in one of its two trials.
  3. Prevention: the biggest lever.COPD kills about 3.2 million people a year, and more than 90% of those deaths are in low- and middle-income countries. Cleaner cooking fuels, cleaner air and smoking cessation would prevent most cases.
  4. Repairing the lung: the long-term hope.No therapy yet regrows damaged air sacs. Lung-regeneration research is early.

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. Treatment decisions belong with a doctor, ideally a pulmonologist, guided by the GOLD report.

Treatments that are working

Inhalers remain the foundation. The big change since 2024 is biologic injections for people who keep having flare-ups despite triple inhaler therapy.

Tozorakimab

FDA priority review
AstraZeneca · anti-IL-33 · OBERON & TITANIA · Sept 8, 2026

In 2,306 people, flare-ups fell 29–34% among former smokers. The effect held across eosinophil levels: 23% fewer flare-ups below 150 and 43% fewer at 300 or above. It's given as an injection every 4 weeks. The FDA decision is expected in Q1 2027, and it's under review in the EU and China.

Dupilumab (Dupixent)

Approved
Sanofi / Regeneron · high-eosinophil COPD · EU Jul 2024, U.S. Sept 2024

The first biologic approved for COPD, first in Europe and then the U.S. and China. It's an add-on for people with raised blood eosinophils.

Mepolizumab (Nucala)

Approved
GSK · eosinophilic COPD · U.S. May 22, 2025

A once-monthly biologic add-on that blocks IL-5. It's also approved in the EU for uncontrolled COPD with raised eosinophils.

Ensifentrine (Ohtuvayre)

Approved
Verona / Merck · nebulized · U.S. June 2024

A first-in-class inhaled treatment that both opens the airways and calms inflammation, for maintenance in adults. It's also approved in Hong Kong and Macau.

Alpha-1 augmentation

Varies by country
For alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Weekly infusions of the missing protein can slow emphysema loss in alpha-1 deficiency. It's available in the U.S. and much of Europe, but England's NICE rejected it as not cost-effective. Longer-acting versions such as INBRX-101 are in trials.

Itepekimab

Mixed Phase 3
Sanofi / Regeneron · anti-IL-33 · AERIFY-2

The rival IL-33 drug missed its main goal in the AERIFY-2 Phase 3 trial. Not every drug aimed at the same target succeeds.

Natural remedies & supplements

No supplement reverses COPD. The most powerful non-drug steps, quitting smoking and pulmonary rehab, have very strong evidence. We rate each by the strength of human evidence.

Good human evidence Observational / mixed No evidence, or possible harm

For lowering risk and slowing decline

Quit smoking (with support)

Slows the loss of lung function at any stage. Counseling plus medicines like varenicline or nicotine replacement work best.

Pulmonary rehab & regular exercise

Improves breathlessness, strength and quality of life, and reduces hospital readmissions after a flare-up.

Vaccines

Flu, pneumococcal, COVID-19 and RSV vaccines prevent the infections that cause many flare-ups.

Supplements people ask about

Vitamin D (if low)

In people with very low levels, supplements reduced flare-ups in pooled trial data. There's no benefit if levels are normal.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC)

High doses modestly reduced flare-ups in some trials, especially in Asia. Results elsewhere are mixed.

Good nutrition & protein

Weight and muscle loss are common in advanced COPD. Nutritional support plus exercise helps.

Herbal "lung cleanses," vaping as a cure

No evidence they repair the lungs. E-cigarettes aren't a treatment for COPD.

Be careful: never smoke, or let anyone smoke, near home oxygen, because of the fire risk. Clinics selling "stem cell" treatments for COPD outside registered trials are unproven and may be unsafe. Tell your care team about everything you take.

What causes COPD

COPD is long-term lung damage that narrows the airways and destroys the tiny air sacs (alveoli). Breathing in smoke, fumes and dust over many years is the main cause, but genes and how well the lungs grew in childhood also matter.

Step 1 · The hits

Years of breathing harm

  • Tobacco smoking: the main cause in wealthy countries
  • Household smoke: cooking and heating with wood, coal or dung, a major cause worldwide
  • Air pollution & work dust: outdoor pollution, mining and farming dusts, chemical fumes
  • Genes & growth: alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, preterm birth, childhood lung infections
Step 2 · The tipping point

Inflammation that won't switch off

  • Immune cells keep attacking the airways even after the exposure stops
  • In some people the inflammation is "type 2" (high eosinophils), and that's who current biologics help
Step 3 · The damage

Airways narrow, air sacs break down

  • Chronic bronchitis: thick mucus and swollen airways
  • Mucus plugs: block small airways
  • Emphysema: air-sac walls are destroyed, trapping air
  • Body-wide effects: heart disease, muscle loss and anxiety often come with it
Step 4 · The result

Breathlessness & flare-ups

  • Shortness of breath, cough and phlegm that slowly worsen
  • Flare-ups (exacerbations) cause hospital stays and lasting decline
  • The third leading cause of death worldwide
Not just smokers: around the world, many people with COPD have never smoked. Household smoke from cooking fuels and outdoor air pollution are major causes, especially in women and in low-income countries.
Mucus is a target (2026): tozorakimab is the first biologic shown to reduce mucus plugging across a broad COPD population, a feature linked to worse outcomes.
Test for alpha-1: the GOLD report recommends testing everyone with COPD once for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, a genetic cause with its own treatments.
