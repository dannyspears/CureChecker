Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no single cure for neuroblastoma, and how a child does depends mostly on their risk group. About 98% of children with low-risk disease and about 95% with intermediate-risk disease are alive five years later, often after surgery alone or modest chemotherapy. In some babies the tumor shrinks away on its own. High-risk neuroblastoma is much harder: even with very intensive treatment, about 6 in 10 children are alive at five years, and the cancer coming back is the main danger. Our view: the biggest recent gains come from immune treatments aimed at GD2, a sugar-like marker on the tumor surface, and from new ways to keep the cancer from returning.

  1. Anti-GD2 antibodies: now part of standard care.In a 226-child randomized trial, adding the antibody dinutuximab after transplant raised 2-year event-free survival from 46% to 66%. Europe uses a close relative, dinutuximab beta.
  2. Keeping it away: a new pill, with debate.The FDA approved eflornithine (Iwilfin) in December 2023 to lower the risk of relapse, based on a comparison with past patients rather than a randomized trial. European regulators were not convinced, and the company withdrew its EU application in July 2025.
  3. CAR-T cells aimed at GD2: strong but still experimental.In final results from Rome (2025), 66% of children with relapsed or resistant disease responded. In children with little disease left, 68% were alive at five years. It is available only in trials.
  4. Matching treatment to the tumor: the next step.About 1 in 10 tumors has an ALK gene change. The ALK drug lorlatinib shrank many of these tumors in early trials, and a large Children's Oncology Group trial is testing it in newly diagnosed children. Its results are not yet reported.

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. Treatment decisions belong with your child's pediatric oncology team, ideally at a center that treats many children with neuroblastoma.

A possible missing link

Please read first. This is an AI-generated hypothesis, written by Cure Checker's AI from published research. It is speculation, not an established finding. It is not a recommendation, not a prediction that a cure exists, and not medical advice. No doctors or scientists have reviewed it, and it may be wrong. Never start, stop, change or delay any treatment because of it. Talk with your pediatric oncologist.

What looks like it is working

  • Targeting GD2. In a randomized trial of 226 children, adding an anti-GD2 antibody raised 2-year event-free survival from 46% to 66% (NEJM, 2010). GD2-directed CAR-T cells produced responses in 66% of children with relapsed or resistant disease (Nat Med, 2025).
  • Finding hidden disease. In 345 children, tumor signals left in bone marrow after early treatment went with lower 5-year event-free survival, 26.6% versus 60.4% (J Exp Clin Cancer Res, 2024).

Where the links are missing

  • Cells that change shape. Neuroblastoma cells can switch between two states, and the "mesenchymal" state resisted chemotherapy in the lab and was more common after treatment (Nat Genet, 2017). One lab study found this switch lowered GD2 (Nat Cancer, 2022), but another, in cells taken from patients, found these cells still carried GD2 (Front Immunol, 2024). Our inference: whether this switch helps tumors escape GD2 treatment in children is unsettled.
  • Escape at relapse. Cells with little or no GD2 but high levels of another marker, B7-H3, were found at relapse in 2 children (J Immunother Cancer, 2021). B7-H3 stays present in both cell states (Neuro-Oncology, 2025). Our inference: a second target might cover cells a GD2 treatment misses.
  • Watching the switch. In 108 children, marrow tests for mesenchymal markers found extra cases, and these markers rose before relapse (J Exp Clin Cancer Res, 2025). Our inference: we found no published study or registered trial that uses repeated tests like these during anti-GD2 treatment to decide when to add a second target, though we may have missed one.

Our hypothesis

Could repeated marrow or blood tests that track both cell states during anti-GD2 treatment show, months before scans can, which children's leftover cells are drifting away from GD2? If so, trials could test adding a second target such as B7-H3 while leftover disease is tiny, using a test strategy that has not been validated.

What would prove it wrong: if rising mesenchymal markers do not come before relapse in larger studies, or if tumors that relapse after anti-GD2 treatment still carry plenty of GD2.

Hypothesis written October 7, 2026. Cure Checker's AI will revisit it when major new evidence appears.

Treatments that are working

Treatment depends on the risk group. Many children with low-risk disease need only surgery or careful watching. High-risk disease needs months of intensive treatment, and that is where most new research is focused.

Anti-GD2 antibodies (dinutuximab, dinutuximab beta)

Approved
High-risk · after transplant · COG ANBL0032 · SIOPEN HR-NBL1

Antibodies latch onto GD2 on tumor cells and call in the immune system. In the U.S. trial, 2-year event-free survival rose from 46% to 66%. Pain during infusions is common and is managed in hospital.

Eflornithine (Iwilfin)

Approved · U.S.
High-risk · after antibody treatment · FDA Dec 13, 2023

A pill taken for up to two years to lower the risk of relapse. Compared with similar past patients, the risk of relapse was about half. It was not a randomized trial, and the EU application was withdrawn in 2025.

Naxitamab (Danyelza)

Accelerated approval · U.S.
Relapsed or resistant disease in bone or marrow · FDA Nov 25, 2020

Another anti-GD2 antibody, given with GM-CSF. In the main study, 45% of patients responded. Continued approval depends on a confirmatory trial.

GD2 CAR-T cells (GD2-CART01)

Promising · Phase 1/2
Bambino Gesù Children's Hospital, Rome · Nature Medicine 2025

A child's own immune cells are engineered to attack GD2. 66% responded, and results were best in children with little disease left. Side effects included cytokine release syndrome, mostly mild.

Lorlatinib for ALK-changed tumors

Early signal · trials
Children's Oncology Group ANBL1531 · not approved for neuroblastoma

An ALK-blocking pill. In an early trial, tumors shrank in 30% of children taking it alone and 63% with chemo. A large trial in newly diagnosed children is still running, and lung problems have been reported when it is given with antibody treatment.

Chemo plus antibody at relapse

Promising · Phase 2
BEACON-Immuno (Europe) · COG ANBL1221 (U.S.)

Adding an anti-GD2 antibody to chemotherapy for relapsed disease. In a randomized European trial, responses were 30.2% versus 18.2%, and the time before the cancer grew again was longer.

Natural remedies & supplements

No supplement, herb or diet cures neuroblastoma or prevents relapse. Some can interfere with chemotherapy or with isotretinoin, which is part of standard care. We rate each by the strength of human evidence. Always check with your child's oncology team or pharmacist first.

Good human evidence Observational / mixed Lab only, or possible harm

Claims you'll see online

Nutrition support with a dietitian

Poor nutrition is common during childhood cancer treatment. A Cochrane review found limited, low-quality evidence that extra feeds help children gain weight. This is part of supportive care, not a tumor treatment.

Exercise & play

Studies in children with leukemia show some fitness gains, but the effects are "not yet convincing." No neuroblastoma-specific trials exist. Ask what is safe during treatment.

Turmeric / curcumin

Neuroblastoma research on curcumin is lab-only. Lab studies suggest it may blunt some chemotherapy drugs, and it changes how the liver processes medicines.

Cannabis / CBD oil

NCI says evidence for cannabis as a cancer treatment in children is limited to a few case reports. It may help nausea or appetite, but CBD oils can change how chemo works.

Use with caution

Vitamin A supplements

Isotretinoin is related to vitamin A. Its FDA label (written for acne) warns against vitamin A supplements because of added toxicity. In neuroblastoma, isotretinoin already causes high calcium in about 1 in 5 children.

High-dose antioxidants & IV vitamin C

Not FDA-approved to treat cancer, with no studies in children. In adults, antioxidant supplements during chemo or radiation have been linked to worse outcomes.

St. John's wort

Cut the active form of irinotecan, a chemo drug used in neuroblastoma, by 42% in a small study. The effect can last weeks after stopping.

Probiotics

Rare bloodstream infections have been reported in people with cancer. A pediatric oncology guideline advises against routine use to prevent C. difficile infection.

Mistletoe

Adult studies have major weaknesses, and there are none in children. NCI says it can't be recommended outside well-designed trials.

Be careful: clinics and websites selling "cures" for childhood cancer can be very costly and may delay treatment that works. The FDA warns that claims like "treats all forms of cancer" or "more effective than chemotherapy" are red flags. Antineoplastons (the Burzynski clinic), once promoted for children's cancers, are not FDA-approved for any disease; the FDA barred new child patients from the program in 2012, and it has been on full FDA hold since 2016. Bring anything you're considering to your child's oncology team, and use the trial registries below to find real trials.

How other countries treat neuroblastoma

The backbone of chemotherapy, surgery, transplant and radiation is similar worldwide. The differences are in immunotherapy and maintenance: the U.S. uses dinutuximab and eflornithine, Europe and the UK use dinutuximab beta, and Japan uses dinutuximab with different helper drugs.

What causes neuroblastoma

Neuroblastoma is a cancer of very young nerve cells, called neuroblasts, that normally help build the body's automatic nervous system before birth. It most often starts in an adrenal gland, above a kidney. Nothing parents did or didn't do causes it. There are no known lifestyle or environmental causes, and only 1 to 2% of cases run in families.

Step 1 · The start

Begins before birth

  • Origin: neural crest cells, which become the nerves that run the body automatically
  • Timing: gene studies show it can begin as early as the first trimester of pregnancy
  • Age: median age at diagnosis is 17 months, and 90% are found before age 5
  • Not caused by: anything parents did. No clear environmental cause is known.
Step 2 · The tipping point

Cells don't finish maturing

  • MYCN: extra copies in 16–25% of tumors and 40–50% of high-risk tumors
  • ALK: changed in about 10% of tumors, and the main cause of the rare inherited form
  • Others: TERT rearrangements, 11q loss, and ATRX changes, which are more common in older children and teens
Step 3 · The damage

How it shows up

  • A lump: most often in the belly, sometimes in the chest, neck or pelvis
  • Spread: often to lymph nodes, bone, bone marrow, liver or skin by diagnosis
  • Signs: bone pain, a swollen belly, bruising around the eyes, bluish skin lumps in babies
  • Babies: some tumors in infants shrink away on their own
Step 4 · The result

Risk group shapes the outlook

  • Five-year survival by risk group, from the NCI
  • Risk depends on age, stage, MYCN, how the cells look under the microscope, and chromosome changes
  • High-risk disease can return, sometimes years later
1983–1984, MYCN: researchers found a gene copied up to 140 times in neuroblastoma cells. A year later, extra copies were found in half of advanced tumors and none of the early-stage ones studied. It is still one of the most important risk markers.
August 24, 2008, ALK: a study showed that inherited ALK gene changes explain most families with neuroblastoma. ALK also turned out to be changed in some non-inherited tumors, which led to ALK-blocking drug trials.
March 27, 2023, before birth: a genetic study found that neuroblastoma of every type can start forming as early as the first trimester of pregnancy, and that how long a tumor has been evolving relates to how it behaves.
