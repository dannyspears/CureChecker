Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no cure for FOP, and bone that has already formed can't be safely removed, because surgery triggers even more bone growth. But 2026 changed the outlook. Three FDA-approved drugs now reduce new bone formation, each blocking the same faulty signal. Our view: the cure will come from shutting that signal down in early childhood, before extra bone builds up, and one day correcting the gene itself.

  1. Blocking the trigger: approved for adults.Garetosmab (Pasatru) blocks activin A, the protein that switches on the faulty receptor. In the OPTIMA trial, people taking it formed 90–94% fewer new bone lesions than those on placebo.
  2. A daily pill for ages 12 and up: newly approved.Zilurgisertib (Atebrioz) directly blocks the overactive receptor, ALK2. It's the first FOP drug approved on Phase 2 data, after the trial showed 81% fewer patients forming new lesions.
  3. Younger children: the next step.Trials of zilurgisertib in children ages 2–12 are recruiting. Starting treatment before bone builds up could protect movement for life.
  4. Fixing the gene: the long-term hope.About 97% of people with FOP share the exact same single-letter DNA change, which makes FOP an unusually clear target for future gene-silencing or gene-editing approaches. That research is still in the lab.

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. FOP care should be guided by an FOP-experienced physician. IFOPA can help connect families with experts.

A possible missing link

Please read first. This is an AI-generated hypothesis, written by Cure Checker's AI from published research. It is speculation, not an established finding. It is not a recommendation, not a prediction that a cure exists, and not medical advice. No doctors or scientists have reviewed it, and it may be wrong. Never start, stop, change or delay any treatment because of it. Talk with your FOP-experienced physician.

What looks like it is working

  • Blocking the trigger. In a Phase 3 trial of 63 adults, there were 19 new bone lesions on placebo versus 1 and 2 on the two doses of garetosmab, which blocks activin A (Lancet, 2026).
  • Blocking the receptor. In the Phase 2 PROGRESS trial, no new lesions were seen through week 48 in 61 people with scans while taking zilurgisertib, though the main 24-week result fell short of statistical significance (HCPLive).
  • Knowing how bone spreads. In FOP mice, new bone tends to form next to old bone, and blocking activin A stopped young lesions from growing (J Bone Miner Res, 2017).
  • Healing without bone. In FOP mice given gene therapy against the faulty receptor, injured muscle made much less bone while normal repair steps largely continued (Nat Commun, 2022).

Where the links are missing

  • Nothing removes the bone already there. The trials measured new bone, not old bone (JBMR Plus, 2026). In the mouse study, activin A was shown to be needed at least until a lesion hardened. Our inference: the bone that already locks joints may not respond to these drugs.
  • Surgery is still off limits. FOP experts say removing extra bone often sets off explosive new bone growth, and advise always avoiding it (JBMR Plus, 2025). Our inference: this advice was written for people not protected by the new drugs, and no one has yet tested whether those drugs change it.
  • No trial asks the question. Our inference: we found no study on ClinicalTrials.gov testing bone removal under drug protection, though we may have missed one.

Our hypothesis

Could a trigger-blocking drug, given before and after an operation, let surgeons remove bone that locks a joint without new bone growing back? If a small, closely watched trial in people already on treatment showed no regrowth on scans a year later, freeing locked joints could become a goal, not just stopping new bone. Today's expert advice to avoid this surgery still stands; the idea should only ever be tested in a carefully run trial.

What would prove it wrong: if bone grows back at the surgery site even with the drug fully blocking the faulty signal, or if wounds fail to heal.

Hypothesis written October 6, 2026. Cure Checker's AI will revisit it when major new evidence appears.

Treatments that are working

Before 2023 there was no approved FOP treatment anywhere in the U.S. Now there are three. They prevent new bone, but they can't remove bone that's already formed.

Zilurgisertib (Atebrioz)

FDA approved
Mirum (from Incyte) · ages 12+ · Sept 25, 2026

A once-daily ALK2-blocking pill. In the PROGRESS trial (63 patients), 81% fewer patients formed new bone lesions at week 24. New bone volume shrank slightly (−3.2 cm³), while it grew by 24.6 cm³ on placebo. U.S. availability is expected in October 2026.

Garetosmab (Pasatru)

FDA approved
Regeneron · adults · Aug 19, 2026

A monthly infusion that blocks activin A. In OPTIMA (63 adults), new bone lesions fell 90–94% and total new bone volume by more than 99% compared with placebo. Clinician-assessed flare-ups also dropped sharply.

Palovarotene (Sohonos)

Approved · U.S. & Canada
Ipsen · girls 8+ and boys 10+ · U.S. Aug 2023

The first FOP drug, a pill that reduces new bone formation. It can cause early closure of growth plates in children, so it's limited by age and needs monitoring. Europe refused it.

Zilurgisertib in young children

Trials recruiting
Ages 2 to under 12

Pediatric cohorts are under way. Treating before bone builds up is the best hope for preserving movement.

Saracatinib

Phase 2
STOPFOP · repurposed cancer drug · Europe

An ALK2-blocking drug originally developed for cancer. Phase 2 results were expected in 2026.

Fidrisertib

Natural remedies & daily care

No supplement or natural remedy slows FOP. The most powerful things families can do are about preventing injury. We rate each by the strength of evidence.

Strong expert consensus Observational / mixed No evidence, or could cause harm

Protecting the body

Avoid muscle injections & biopsies

Use other routes for vaccines and medicines when possible. Biopsies of FOP lumps can trigger new bone.

Fall prevention

Soft surfaces, home modifications and helmets for children reduce the injuries that start flare-ups.

Careful dental care

Jaw stretching and nerve-block injections can lock the jaw. Choose a dentist who follows FOP guidance.

What people ask about

Gentle activity & water therapy

Low-impact movement and warm-water therapy can support well-being. Avoid forceful stretching or massage of stiff areas.

Normal calcium & vitamin D

Healthy intake supports normal bone. There's no evidence that limiting or boosting them changes extra bone formation.

"Bone-dissolving" supplements

No supplement removes FOP bone. Don't take high-dose vitamin A products (palovarotene is a vitamin A-like drug).

Be careful: surgery to remove extra bone usually causes more bone to form. Any operation or dental work should be planned with an FOP expert. Carry an FOP medical alert card.

What causes FOP

FOP is caused by a change in one gene, ACVR1. It makes a bone-signaling receptor overactive, so after an injury or "flare-up," muscles, tendons and ligaments slowly turn into bone. Over time, a second skeleton locks joints in place.

Step 1 · The gene

One letter changed

~97% share the same change (R206H)
  • Usually new: most cases are a new change, not inherited, but a parent with FOP can pass it on (a 50% chance)
  • Rare: about 3,500–4,000 people worldwide
  • Clue at birth: short, bent big toes are an early telltale sign
Step 2 · The tipping point

Activin A flips the wrong switch

  • Normally activin A doesn't activate ACVR1. In FOP it does, sending a constant "make bone" signal.
  • That's exactly what the new drugs block
Step 3 · The damage

Flare-ups build new bone

  • Triggers: bumps, falls, muscle injections, dental injections, viral illness, or sometimes nothing obvious
  • Flare-up: painful swelling, then the tissue turns to cartilage and bone over weeks
  • Surgery backfires: removing extra bone causes more to form
Step 4 · The result

A second skeleton

  • Neck, back and shoulders are usually affected first, then the hips and limbs
  • Bone around the chest can restrict breathing
  • Median life expectancy has been in the mid-50s
Three drugs, one pathway: palovarotene, garetosmab and zilurgisertib all act on the same faulty ACVR1 signal, at different points.
Avoiding triggers still matters: no muscle injections (when possible), no biopsies, careful dental work and fall prevention. These protect people even on treatment.
Misdiagnosis is common: FOP lumps are often mistaken for cancer, and a biopsy can trigger new bone. Short, bent big toes plus soft-tissue swellings should prompt a genetic test.
