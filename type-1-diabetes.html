Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no cure for type 1 diabetes (T1D). The immune system destroys the cells in the pancreas that make insulin, so most people need insulin every day to stay alive. One drug can now change the course of the disease: teplizumab (Tzield) can delay diagnosis in people in the early, symptom-free stage. Since June 12, 2026, in the U.S., it can also slow the loss of the body's own insulin in children 8 to 17 who were recently diagnosed. The closest thing to a cure is islet cell replacement. In a small trial, Vertex's lab-grown cells (zimislecel) let 10 of 12 people stop insulin, but everyone needs lifelong immune-suppressing drugs. Our view: a functional cure for some people looks reachable. A cure without immune suppression is still at the one-patient stage.

  1. A delay, not a cure.Teplizumab, a 14-day infusion, delayed stage 3 T1D by a median of about two years in its key trial. In the U.S. it is approved for stage 2 from age 1 (since April 20, 2026) and for recently diagnosed children 8 to 17 (accelerated approval, June 12, 2026).
  2. Cell therapy works in a small trial.In the NEJM zimislecel study, 10 of 12 people needed no insulin at one year, and none had severe low blood sugar. Two people died, one from a fungal brain infection (cryptococcal meningitis) and one from worsening dementia they already had.
  3. Timing is uncertain.Vertex had planned to file zimislecel for approval in 2026. It paused dosing for a manufacturing review, then resumed it. On August 3, 2026 it said updated timelines will come "later this year." Zimislecel is not approved anywhere.
  4. Not every idea works.In 2026, high-dose oral insulin did not slow early T1D in children (Fr1da trial). In low-resource settings, glargine insulin did no better than older human insulin (HumAn-1).

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. Care decisions belong with a diabetes care team.

Gum-tissue stem cells: small Phase 1 trial

Sep 17, 2026

FDA approves finerenone (Kerendia) for kidney disease linked to type 1 diabetes

The daily pill is the first new treatment approved in more than 30 years for kidney disease in adults with type 1 diabetes. In the 242-person FINE-ONE trial, a kidney-damage marker (UACR) was 22% lower than on placebo at 3 months and 28% lower at 6 months (company-reported). Overall side effects were similar to placebo, but high blood potassium was more common (10.1% vs 3.3%). It treats kidney disease, not diabetes itself.

Treatments that are working

Insulin keeps people alive. Devices, an immune drug and islet cell therapies are changing what treatment looks like.

Insulin

Standard
Everyone with stage 3 T1D · injections or pump

Replaces the hormone the body no longer makes. Most people with T1D need it every day to stay alive. Missing doses can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis, a life-threatening emergency.

Automated insulin delivery

Proven
Pump + glucose sensor + software · RADIANT trial

The system adjusts insulin based on sensor readings. In the RADIANT trial (188 people aged 4 to 70), a tubeless system lowered average blood sugar (HbA1c) from 8.1% to 7.2% in 13 weeks, compared with 8.1% to 8.0% on injections. The trial was funded by the device maker.

Tzield (teplizumab)

FDA approved
First approved Nov 17, 2022 · stage 2 from age 1 · recent stage 3, ages 8–17

An antibody given by IV for 12 to 14 days. It delays the onset of stage 3 T1D and slows the loss of the body's own insulin. It carries a boxed warning for serious viral reactivation (Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus).

Lantidra (donislecel)

FDA approved
Approved Jun 28, 2023 · adults with repeated severe low blood sugar

Islet cells from deceased donors, infused into a liver vein. In two small studies, 21 of 30 people stopped insulin for at least a year. Patients must take immune-suppressing drugs, and some stopped because of serious side effects.

Zimislecel (VX-880)

In Phase 3
Vertex · lab-grown islet cells · FORWARD trial

Islet cells grown from stem cells. In NEJM, 10 of 12 full-dose patients were off insulin at one year. Lifelong immune suppression is required, and two patients died during the study. Not approved.

Hypoimmune islet cells

First patient
Sana Biotechnology · Uppsala University Hospital, Sweden

Donor islet cells gene-edited to hide from the immune system. In one person, they kept making insulin for 14 months with no immune-suppressing drugs. This is a single patient, and much larger trials are needed.

Natural remedies & daily care

No supplement or diet replaces insulin in type 1 diabetes. Daily habits help manage blood sugar alongside insulin. We rate each by the strength of evidence.

Strong expert consensus Limited / mixed evidence No evidence, or possible harm

Helpful daily care

Regular physical activity

Can help lower blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol. Ask your care team how to adjust insulin and food around exercise.

Healthy eating plan

A plan built with a dietitian or diabetes educator helps match food and insulin.

Sleep and stress care

Part of diabetes self-care, though trial evidence for better blood sugar is limited.

Promoted without evidence, or risky

Cinnamon, chromium, berberine

Studies are small and inconsistent, with no clear conclusions. None replaces insulin.

"Natural diabetes cure" products

The FDA warns these are marketed illegally. Some are harmful, and all are harmful if used in place of real treatment.

Stopping insulin for any remedy

Missing insulin can cause diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which is life-threatening.

Be careful: never stop or cut insulin to try a supplement, diet or "cure." In type 1 diabetes, going without insulin can cause diabetic ketoacidosis, a life-threatening emergency. Some supplements can also cause side effects, such as kidney damage. Tell your care team about anything you take.

What causes type 1 diabetes

T1D is an autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks and destroys the beta cells in the pancreas that make insulin. Experts now describe it in stages that start years before symptoms.

Step 1 · Stage 1

The immune attack starts

~9 million people(WHO, 2017)
  • Blood test sign: two or more islet autoantibodies
  • Blood sugar: still normal
  • Symptoms: none
Step 2 · The tipping point

Stage 2: blood sugar drifts

  • Autoantibodies plus abnormal blood sugar, still with no symptoms
  • This is the stage where teplizumab can delay diagnosis
Step 3 · The damage

Stage 3: symptoms appear

  • Diagnosis: too little insulin, so blood sugar climbs
  • Emergency risk: diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is sometimes the first sign
  • Treatment: insulin starts
Step 4 · The result

Lifelong insulin

  • Most people need insulin every day to stay alive
  • Good blood-sugar management can prevent or delay health problems
Stages, not a sudden start (2015): JDRF (now Breakthrough T1D), the Endocrine Society and the American Diabetes Association defined stages 1 to 3, so the disease can be found and treated before symptoms.
T cells drive it (2019): the TN-10 trial showed that teplizumab, an antibody aimed at T cells, delayed diagnosis in high-risk relatives. Calming those immune cells changes the course of the disease.
Training the immune system is hard (2026): in the Fr1da trial, a year of high-dose oral insulin given to children in stage 1 did not slow progression or change their immune response to insulin.
