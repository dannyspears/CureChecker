Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no cure for type 2 diabetes. The CDC estimates that 40.1 million people in the U.S. had diabetes in 2023, diagnosed or not, and says about 90% to 95% of people with diabetes have type 2. Some people can reach remission. A 2021 international expert consensus defines it as an HbA1c below 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) at least 3 months after glucose-lowering medicine has stopped. Remission is not a cure: diabetes can come back, so regular checks continue. In the UK DiRECT trial, adults diagnosed within the past 6 years followed a structured weight-loss program with medicines withdrawn by their care teams. 46% were in remission at 1 year and 36% at 2 years. By 5 years, 13% of those who kept getting support were still in remission. Our view: the biggest recent gains are medicines that protect the heart and kidneys and help with large weight loss, plus proof that remission is possible for some people, mostly early after diagnosis.

  1. GLP-1-based medicines: strong effects on sugar and weight.In a 1,879-person trial, tirzepatide (a GIP and GLP-1 drug) lowered HbA1c more than semaglutide and led to 1.9 to 5.5 kg more weight loss (Lilly-funded). The experimental triple-hormone drug retatrutide led to average weight loss of up to 18.8% at 80 weeks in people with type 2 diabetes (Lilly-funded); it is not FDA-approved.
  2. Remission through weight loss: real, but hard to keep.In DiRECT, remission rose with weight lost: 86% of people who lost 15 kg (33 lb) or more were in remission at 1 year, but none of those who gained weight were. Of those in remission at 2 years, 26% were still in remission at 5 years.
  3. Protecting the heart and kidneys.In people with type 2 diabetes and kidney disease, the SGLT2 drug canagliflozin lowered the risk of kidney failure, worsening kidney function, or death from kidney or heart causes by 30% (CREDENCE, 2019). Semaglutide lowered a similar combined risk by 24% (FLOW, 2024). Both trials were company-funded.
  4. Fewer shots, and new pills in testing.The FDA approved the first once-weekly insulins for adults with type 2 diabetes: Awiqli (March 26, 2026) and Onswik (September 23, 2026). In trials they lowered HbA1c about as well as daily insulin. The oral GLP-1 pill orforglipron lowered HbA1c in early type 2 diabetes, but its FDA approval (April 1, 2026) is for weight management only.

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. Never stop or change a diabetes medicine on your own. Treatment decisions belong with your diabetes care team.

A possible missing link

Please read first. This is an AI-generated hypothesis, written by Cure Checker's AI from published research. It is speculation, not an established finding. It is not a recommendation, not a prediction that a cure exists, and not medical advice. No doctors or scientists have reviewed it, and it may be wrong. Never start, stop, change or delay any treatment because of it. Talk with your diabetes care team.

What looks like it is working

  • Large weight loss. In DiRECT, 86% of people who lost 15 kg or more were in remission at 1 year, compared with 7% of those who lost 0 to 5 kg (Lancet, 2018).
  • Insulin-making cells can recover. In 11 people on an 8-week very-low-calorie diet, insulin release and the liver's response to insulin returned to normal as fat in the liver and pancreas fell (Diabetologia, 2011). In a DiRECT subgroup, the return of the fast "first-phase" insulin response marked who reached normal blood sugar (Cell Metab, 2018).

Where the links are missing

  • Same fat loss, different outcome. In that subgroup, liver and pancreas fat fell whether or not blood sugar returned to normal. People who responded looked similar at the start, except they had had diabetes for less time (2.7 vs 3.8 years) (Cell Metab, 2018). Our inference: no test has been shown to tell, ahead of time, whose insulin-making cells can still recover.
  • Relapse follows fat returning. People who relapsed regained pancreas fat and lost the first-phase response by 24 months (Cell Metab, 2020). Only 26% of people in remission at 2 years were still in remission at 5 years (Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol, 2024).
  • Medicines versus diet. A 16-week program using an insulin and liraglutide combination, with 3.3% average weight loss, did not lead to lasting remission (Diabetes Obes Metab, 2024). A pooled analysis found tirzepatide raised the odds of near-normal HbA1c more than 16-fold while people were taking it (J Diabetes Complications, 2024). Our inference: normal blood sugar while on a medicine is not remission, and we found no large trial that measured whether the same cell recovery seen after diet-driven weight loss also follows medicine-driven weight loss, though we may have missed one.

Our hypothesis

Could measuring the first-phase insulin response, or pancreas fat, after large weight loss (however that weight loss was reached) show which people have regained enough insulin-making ability to stay in remission, better than how long they have had diabetes? If so, researchers could design remission trials around it. No such validated test exists for patients today; this is an idea for research to test.

What would prove it wrong: if, in larger studies, the first-phase insulin response measured after weight loss does not predict who stays in remission better than the amount of weight lost and the years since diagnosis, or if people whose response recovered relapse just as often as people whose response did not.

SGLT2 inhibitors and heart and kidney outcomes: new pooled analysis of 14 trials

A meta-analysis of 14 placebo-controlled trials (76,969 adults with type 2 diabetes) found SGLT2 inhibitors (such as empagliflozin and dapagliflozin) cut the combined risk of heart-failure hospitalization or cardiovascular death by 23% and kidney outcomes by 31%, with no clear effect on stroke. The authors note differences between trials, including how kidney outcomes were defined. It pools existing trials rather than testing anything new.

Treatments that are working

Type 2 diabetes can be controlled for many years, and newer medicines now do more than lower blood sugar. Some also protect the heart and kidneys or bring large weight loss. A small number of people reach remission (normal blood sugar without diabetes medicines) after major weight loss, but blood sugar can rise again, so it is not a cure.

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro)

Approved
Weekly shot · GIP and GLP-1 · SURPASS-2 · SURPASS-CVOT · FDA May 13, 2022

It acts on two gut hormones that help control blood sugar and appetite. In a head-to-head trial, A1C fell 2.01 to 2.30 points, compared with 1.86 on semaglutide, and people lost more weight. In a large heart trial of 13,165 adults with heart disease, heart attack, stroke or heart-related death happened in 12.2% versus 13.1% on dulaglutide. That showed it was at least as good, but not clearly better. Stomach side effects were more common.

Semaglutide (Ozempic shot, Rybelsus pill)

Approved
GLP-1 · FLOW · SOUL · FDA Dec 5, 2017 (shot) · FDA Sep 20, 2019 (pill)

In people with diabetes and kidney disease (FLOW), the weekly shot lowered the risk of serious kidney events by 24% and death from any cause by 20%. In people with heart or kidney disease (SOUL), the daily pill cut major heart events to 12.0% from 13.8% on placebo over about 4 years.

SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin and others)

Approved
Daily pills · EMPA-REG OUTCOME · DECLARE-TIMI 58 · FDA Aug 1, 2014 (empagliflozin)

These pills make the kidneys pass extra sugar in the urine. In EMPA-REG OUTCOME, deaths from heart disease fell to 3.7% from 5.9%, and hospital stays for heart failure fell to 2.7% from 4.1%. DECLARE found fewer heart-failure hospital stays and kidney events, but no drop in heart attacks or strokes. Genital infections are more common.

Orforglipron (Foundayo), a GLP-1 pill

Approved for weight · U.S.
ACHIEVE-1 · FDA Apr 1, 2026 (weight management only) · UK diabetes approval Aug 10, 2026

A daily pill that is not a protein, unlike earlier GLP-1 medicines. In people with early type 2 diabetes, A1C fell 1.24 to 1.48 points over 40 weeks, compared with 0.41 on placebo, and weight fell up to 7.6%. In the U.S. it is approved only for weight management so far. The UK has authorised it for type 2 diabetes.

Retatrutide

Promising · Phase 3
Eli Lilly · TRIUMPH-2 (The Lancet, Sep 29, 2026) · TRANSCEND-T2D-1 · not approved

A weekly shot that acts on three hormone receptors (GIP, GLP-1 and glucagon). In adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes, weight fell 11.9% to 18.8% over 80 weeks, compared with 5.1% on placebo. A second trial found A1C drops of 1.7 to 2.0 points, but those results are company-reported and not yet in a journal. Up to 12% stopped treatment because of side effects.

Remission through major weight loss

Proven in trials
DiRECT · UK family doctors · The Lancet 2017 · 5-year follow-up 2024

A medically supervised program of meal-replacement shakes for 3 to 5 months, then slow return to food. After a year, 46% were in remission, compared with 4% with usual care, and 86% of those who lost 15 kg (33 lb) or more were in remission. Remission often faded: at 5 years, 13% of those still in the program were in remission.

Natural remedies & supplements

No supplement, herb or diet product cures type 2 diabetes. Some interact with diabetes medicines, and some can push blood sugar too low. We rate each by the strength of human evidence. Ask your care team or pharmacist before you try anything, and never stop a prescribed medicine on your own.

Claims you'll see online

Structured diet & weight loss

The best-tested "natural" approach. In the DiRECT trial, a supervised weight-loss program put 46% of people into remission at 1 year and 36% at 2 years, compared with 4% and 3% on usual care. Remission depended on keeping the weight off, and this was done with medical supervision, not alone.

Regular physical activity

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) says structured exercise programs of at least 8 weeks lowered A1C by about 0.66% in type 2 diabetes. Its 2026 standards advise most adults to get 150 minutes or more of moderate activity a week, plus strength training. Ask your team what is safe for you.

Cinnamon

A Cochrane review found no clear effect on A1C. A later review found lower fasting blood sugar, but the studies differed a lot. The ADA does not recommend cinnamon for blood sugar. Cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which in large amounts might worsen liver disease.

Berberine

A 2021 review of 46 studies suggested it may lower blood sugar, but most studies were done in China and some were poor quality. It can cause stomach upset and may interact with medicines. It should not be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or given to infants.

Apple cider vinegar

A 2025 review pooled only 7 small trials and reported lower fasting blood sugar. Larger, better trials are needed before anyone can say it helps.

Vitamin D

In the D2d trial of 2,423 adults with prediabetes, vitamin D did not significantly lower the risk of getting type 2 diabetes. The ADA does not advise routine vitamin D for people with diabetes who are not low in it. Too much can be harmful.

Use with caution

Chromium

A 2022 review of 16 small studies suggested it may help blood sugar, but the ADA does not recommend it. A few reports link large doses to kidney, liver, muscle and skin problems. Long-term effects are not well studied.

Magnesium

People who eat more magnesium tend to have lower diabetes risk, but a 2022 review said research on supplements is not enough to guide care. The ADA does not recommend them for blood sugar. Large doses cause diarrhea and cramps, and very large doses can be deadly.

Bitter melon

Human studies are few and low quality. Memorial Sloan Kettering warns it may add to the effect of insulin and other diabetes medicines. Case reports include stomach ulcers and severe kidney injury.

Fenugreek

Some studies suggest it may lower blood sugar, but many were not high quality. NCCIH says large doses may cause a harmful drop in blood sugar. It may raise bleeding risk with warfarin and can cause serious allergic reactions.

Aloe vera (by mouth)

Only a small amount of research suggests it may lower blood sugar, and the ADA does not recommend it. Aloe leaf extracts taken by mouth have been linked to cases of acute hepatitis, and aloe latex can cause cramps and diarrhea.

Be careful: the FDA warns against products that claim to be a "natural diabetes cure" or to "replace your diabetes medicine." They are sold illegally. Some "blood sugar support" products sold as supplements contained hidden prescription diabetes drugs. FDA tests found glyburide and metformin in Dr. Ergin's SugarMD Advanced Glucose Support (Nov 3, 2023) and in Fouzee SugarLin Herbal Formula (Dec 6, 2024). Hidden drugs like these can cause dangerously low blood sugar. The FDA also warns that unapproved versions of GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide are not reviewed for safety or quality. Some carry fake pharmacy labels, some are sold "for research use," and counterfeit Ozempic pens have been found in the U.S. drug supply (most recently Dec 5, 2025). Get these medicines only with a prescription from a state-licensed pharmacy. Herbs that lower blood sugar, such as bitter melon or fenugreek, may add to insulin or other diabetes medicines and cause lows. Tell your care team about every supplement you take, and never stop or cut a prescribed medicine to try one.

How other countries treat type 2 diabetes

The basics are the same almost everywhere: healthy habits, metformin, SGLT2 and GLP-1 medicines, and insulin. The differences are in newer medicines. Japan uses imeglimin and China uses dorzagliatin, neither sold in the U.S. A weekly insulin reached Europe, Canada, Japan and China two years before the U.S., and the UK approved the orforglipron pill for diabetes first.

What causes type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes happens when the body stops responding well to insulin and the pancreas can no longer make enough insulin to keep blood sugar in a healthy range. It is not a personal failing. Researchers still don't fully know what starts it. Age, family history and genes, extra weight, low physical activity, and social factors such as income, neighborhood, the local food environment and access to health care all play roles.

Step 1 · The start

Risk builds over years

  • Who is at risk: people 45 or older, with a parent, brother or sister with type 2 diabetes, with extra weight, or active fewer than 3 times a week
  • Genes: a common TCF7L2 gene variant raises risk about 1.5-fold with one copy and 2.4-fold with two
  • Life circumstances: diabetes affects low-income adults and some racial and ethnic groups more in the U.S.
  • Younger people: more and more children, teens and young adults are developing it
Step 2 · The tipping point

Insulin stops keeping up

  • Insulin resistance: muscle, fat and liver cells stop responding well to insulin
  • Organ fat: extra fat in the liver and pancreas is thought to drive both problems, and can fall with large weight loss
  • The pancreas: over time it cannot make enough insulin, and blood sugar rises
  • Prediabetes first: an estimated 115.2 million U.S. adults have prediabetes
Step 3 · The damage

Quiet harm over time

  • Often hidden: about 27.6% of U.S. adults with diabetes, 11.0 million people, don't know they have it
  • Blood vessels: damage can lead to heart disease and stroke
  • Kidneys, eyes, nerves: kidney disease, vision loss and nerve damage
  • Feet: nerve damage and poor blood flow can cause serious foot problems
Step 4 · The result

Lifelong, but sometimes reversible

  • Share in remission at 1 year by kilograms lost, across everyone in the DiRECT trial (Lancet, 2018)
  • Works best early: people who reached remission had had diabetes for less time
  • Can come back: regaining weight brought back pancreas fat and diabetes in some people, so checkups continue
January 1936, two kinds of diabetes: Harold Himsworth wrote in The Lancet that diabetes could be split into "insulin-sensitive" and "insulin-insensitive" types. He proposed that some diabetes comes from the body not responding to insulin, rather than from a lack of insulin, an early form of the idea now called insulin resistance.
January 15, 2006, a gene variant: a study in Nature Genetics found that a common variant in the TCF7L2 gene raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, a finding confirmed in Icelandic, Danish and U.S. groups. The authors estimated it accounted for about 21% of type 2 diabetes risk in the groups studied.
June 9, 2011, reversible in some: in a small Newcastle study of 11 people, 8 weeks of a very-low-calorie diet brought insulin release and the liver's insulin response back to normal as liver and pancreas fat fell. The same Newcastle team later helped run the DiRECT remission trial.
