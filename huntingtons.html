Cure Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Most recent cure information

There is no cure for Huntington's disease, and every approved drug treats symptoms only. But Huntington's is caused by one known gene, which makes it one of the most targetable brain diseases. Our view: the cure will come from lowering the toxic huntingtin protein early, before symptoms, with a one-time gene therapy or a daily pill.

  1. One-time gene therapy: filed for approval.uniQure's AMT-130 slowed decline by 75% over three years at the high dose, compared with matched patients from outside the trial. It was filed with the FDA and the UK in September 2026.
  2. Pills that lower huntingtin: catching up.Votoplam (Novartis/PTC) has entered a roughly 770-person Phase 3, and Skyhawk's SKY-0515 showed a benefit at 15 months. A pill would be far easier to reach than brain surgery.
  3. Not every approach works: a 2026 reality check.Roche ended its tominersen program in July 2026 after it lowered huntingtin but didn't improve symptoms. Timing and dose seem to matter as much as the target.
  4. Starting before symptoms: the long-term goal.A genetic test can identify people who will develop Huntington's decades ahead of time. Prevention trials in gene carriers could one day stop the disease before it starts.

This is Cure Checker's editorial assessment of published evidence, not medical advice. Treatment and genetic-testing decisions belong with a neurologist and a genetic counselor, ideally at an HDSA Center of Excellence.

Treatments that are working

Today's approved drugs only calm movements and mood. The new wave aims at the gene itself. Some are showing the first signs of actually slowing the disease, though these are early, small results.

AMT-130 (ifezuntirgene inilparvovec)

Filed for approval
uniQure · one-time gene therapy · BLA & UK MAA Sept 2026

Injected once into the brain to permanently lower huntingtin. At three years, the high dose showed 75% slower decline than matched outside patients, plus lower NfL. The trials were small (dozens of patients). The FDA agreed in June 2026 that these data could support accelerated approval.

Votoplam

Promising · Phase 3
PTC / Novartis · daily pill · Apr 2026

At 24 months, decline slowed by 52% (10 mg) and 28% (5 mg) compared with a natural-history group. Novartis started the INVEST-HD Phase 3, aiming for about 770 people.

SKY-0515

Promising · Phase 1/2
Skyhawk · daily pill · Sept 15, 2026

Lowered mutant huntingtin in blood by up to 62% and showed a 1.59-point benefit on the main Huntington's rating scale at 15 months, compared with an outside control group.

Valbenazine (Ingrezza)

FDA approved
Neurocrine · chorea · Aug 2023

The newest of three VMAT2 drugs that calm involuntary movements. It treats a symptom and doesn't slow the disease.

Tominersen

Discontinued
Roche · antisense drug · July 9, 2026

The GENERATION HD2 trial met its safety and biomarker goals but didn't show meaningful benefit over placebo, so Roche ended the program.

Pridopidine

Not approved in EU
Prilenia / Ferrer · Europe 2025

Europe's medicines committee advised against approval in July 2025, and the company withdrew its application in November 2025 to collect more data.

Natural remedies & supplements

No supplement prevents or slows Huntington's. Several were tested in large trials and failed. Nutrition and exercise still matter for quality of life. We rate each by the strength of human evidence.

Good human evidence Observational / mixed Tested, did not work

For daily life

Exercise & physical therapy

Small trials suggest better balance, fitness and mood. It's safe and widely recommended. Work with a therapist who knows Huntington's.

High-calorie nutrition

Many people with Huntington's lose weight despite eating well, and weight loss tracks with faster decline. A dietitian can help with calories and safe swallowing.

Mediterranean-style diet

Linked to later symptom onset in observational studies. It isn't proof, but it's a healthy choice anyway.

Tested and failed

Creatine (high dose)

A large trial in early Huntington's was stopped for futility.

Coenzyme Q10

A large Phase 3 trial (2CARE) found no slowing of decline.

Ethyl-EPA (omega-3)

Controlled trials didn't show a benefit on Huntington's symptoms.

Be careful: chorea drugs (VMAT2 inhibitors) can worsen depression and interact with other medicines. Some supplements claim to "lower huntingtin" with no evidence at all. Tell your care team about everything you take.

What causes Huntington's

Huntington's is caused by a single faulty gene, HTT. A stretch of its DNA, the three letters "CAG," repeats too many times. The result is a toxic form of the huntingtin protein that slowly damages brain cells, starting in a region that controls movement and mood.

Step 1 · The gene

An inherited repeat

50% chancefor each child of a carrier
  • Dominant: one copy is enough, and each child of a carrier has a 1 in 2 chance of inheriting it
  • The count: 40+ CAG repeats nearly always causes disease. 36–39 may or may not.
  • Early onset: very long repeats (often 60+) cause juvenile Huntington's, which starts before age 20
Step 2 · The tipping point

The repeat keeps growing in brain cells

  • In vulnerable neurons, the repeat lengthens over a lifetime ("somatic expansion"). Genes that control this, like MSH3 and FAN1, change when symptoms begin.
  • Long repeats make a toxic huntingtin protein
Step 3 · The damage

The striatum wears away

  • Vulnerable cells: medium spiny neurons in the striatum die first
  • Clumps: pieces of huntingtin stick together inside cells
  • Energy and transport fail: cells can't supply or clean themselves
  • Spreads: the cortex and other regions follow
Step 4 · The result

Movement, mind and mood change

  • Involuntary movements (chorea), clumsiness and trouble swallowing
  • Slower thinking and planning, depression and irritability
  • Symptoms usually start between 30 and 50, and the disease runs 15–20 years
Huntingtin lowering works on biomarkers: several 2026 drugs cut huntingtin and the nerve-damage marker NfL. The open question is how much lowering, and how early, actually changes the course of the disease.
Somatic expansion: growth of the CAG repeat inside brain cells is now a leading drug target. Slowing it could delay symptoms for decades.
Early-onset (juvenile) Huntington's: about 5–10% of cases begin before age 20, usually inherited from the father. Stiffness and seizures are more common than chorea.
